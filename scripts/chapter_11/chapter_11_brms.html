We start with the simple intercept-only logistic regression model, which follows the statistical formula

\[ \begin{align} \text{pulled_left}_{i} & \sim \text{Binomial}(1, p_{i}) \\ \text{logit}(p_{i}) & = \alpha \\ \alpha & \sim \text{Normal}(0, w), \end{align} \] where \(w\) is the parameter for the \(\sigma\) the value for which we have yet to choose. To start things off, we’ll set \(w\) = 10, fit a model with where we set sample_prior = TRUE, and get a sense of the prior on a plot.

11.2.1.1 Overthinking: Modeling tool innovation.

Scientific model for total_tools is \[ \text{total_tools} = \frac{\alpha_{\text{cid}[i]}\text{population}^{\beta_{cid}[i]}}{\gamma}. \]

Within the context of the Poisson likelihood, we can decompose \(\lambda\) into two parts, \(\mu\) (mean) and \(\tau\) (exposure), like this: \[ \begin{align} y_{i} & \sim Poisson(\lambda_{i}) \\ \text{log}\lambda_{i} & = \text{log} \frac{\mu_{i}}{\tau_{i}} = \text{log} \ \mu_{i} - \text{log} \ \tau_{i}. \end{align} \]

You can rewrite the equation if the exposure varies in your data and you still want to model the mean. Here’s what that might look like:

\[ \begin{align} y_{i} & \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu_i) \\ \text{log}\mu_i & = \text{log} \ \tau_i + \alpha + \beta \text{monastery}_i \\ \alpha & \sim \text{Normal}(0, 1) \\ \beta & \sim \text{Normal}(0, 1), \end{align} \]
